The conservative case is also right that the capex story is not yet fully proven. Affordability does not remove market risk. AMZN can pay for the buildout, but the market may still compress the multiple if it believes AMZN is spending ahead of returns. The bear case is real: negative Q1 free cash flow, rising capex, rising debt, a growing depreciation base, and headline earnings flattered by 15.7 billion in security sale gains. AMZN’s reported net income is not a clean run-rate signal. Total debt is up 57.6% year over year, net PPE is about 53% of total assets, and the balance sheet is becoming more asset-heavy. That makes AMZN more sensitive than the aggressive case admits to whether infrastructure spending converts into AWS, advertising, Prime, and retail economics. The one-time gain argues against full-size commitment, not against controlled participation.

The headline risk stack also supports a smaller starter position. The 2.5 billion Prime settlement is bounded, but it lands while AMZN free cash flow is already negative and while Prime workforce practices remain under a compliance overhang. Berkshire exiting AMZN is not a fundamental breakdown, but it removes a high-visibility long-term holder from the narrative and can act as a sentiment tax. The Seattle data-center moratorium is the most important early-warning item because it is a first-of-its-kind regulatory friction attached directly to the infrastructure build. It is not yet systemic, but if it spreads to other major tech hubs, it becomes a structural capacity-timing risk. Engineer protests and labor friction are not earnings breakers today, but they are execution-risk monitors for a company whose moat depends heavily on engineering velocity.

Prime Day is the key reason the AMZN trade must be event-aware. The aggressive case correctly treats Prime Day as a potential catalyst: it is dated, it front-loads Q2 revenue and membership activity, and it can provide evidence of consumer demand resilience. But the conservative case is also right that Prime Day is two-way. If AMZN shows strong, non-discount-dependent demand, the stock has a path to reclaim 251.16 and move toward 265 and 278.56. If Prime Day coverage is muted, discount-dependent, or followed by a broader tech wobble, AMZN can fall through the planned support. The balanced solution is not to stand flat, but to own a meaningful starter position, keep dry powder, and let the event help prove the case.

The neutral case provides the best execution framework. It correctly rejects a full hold because the 200-SMA trend, AMZN liquidity, and catalyst calendar are too important to ignore. It also correctly rejects the original aggressive plan because the initial size is too large and the tape is not confirmed. The final AMZN decision therefore adopts the neutral structure: start smaller, use 239 as an immediate reduction line, keep 232.13 as the full invalidation line, limit pre-Prime Day exposure, and require proof before scaling.

The past-mistake lesson in the research plan is the deciding factor: do not let a long-term quality thesis override technical invalidation and position sizing. AMZN is a high-quality franchise, but quality does not remove the need for staged entries, hard stops, and event-aware risk control. This plan corrects that by making the Buy on AMZN conditional: the 200-SMA is the line in the sand, 239 is the warning line, Prime Day is a two-way catalyst, and full size is earned only after AWS, capex guidance, and technical structure confirm the thesis.

A Sell on AMZN is not justified here because the long-term trend has not broken, the balance sheet is not distressed, and there is no confirmed failure in AWS monetization or consumer demand. A Hold would be too passive because AMZN still has a defined support structure, a funded capex cycle, and dated catalysts that can create meaningful upside if the correction matures. The right decision is therefore a **Buy on AMZN**, executed with smaller initial size, tighter pre-event exposure, proof-based adds, and hard invalidation levels.
